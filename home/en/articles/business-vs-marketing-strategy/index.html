Business strategy vs marketing strategy

Rima Yaqub, November 2026, 8 min read

When I ask an owner, “What’s your strategy?”, I usually hear about Instagram, ads and how many posts go out each week. That is a marketing plan, and it matters. But it isn’t the business strategy. Confusing the two is one of the most common reasons good businesses work very hard and earn very little.

Why we confuse them

Because marketing is the part we can see. The post goes out, the ad runs, messages arrive. Strategy is a set of quiet decisions nobody sees: who do we work for? What do we sell? And how does the business profit from it?

Because those decisions are hard, we postpone them and jump straight to whatever feels like movement. So we market everything to everyone, change the message every week, and then ask why marketing isn’t working.

Business strategy: where to play and how to win

In Playing to Win, A.G. Lafley and Roger Martin define strategy as a set of integrated choices, the most important being two questions: where will we play, and how will we win there? In other words, which client we serve, and what makes us the best choice for them.

Michael Porter goes further. In his well-known Harvard Business Review article “What Is Strategy?” (1996), he writes that the essence of strategy is choosing what not to do. A business that tries to serve everyone, with every service, at every price, doesn’t have a strategy. It has a crowded calendar.

In a service business, strategy answers questions like: which service is the heart of the business? Which client do we want more of, and which one isn’t a fit? How do we price? And what makes income repeat instead of starting from zero every month?

Marketing strategy: how to reach the client we chose

Marketing strategy comes next. In Philip Kotler’s model it starts with three steps: segment the market, choose the target segment, then position the offer in that client’s mind. Only then come channels, content and ads.

Put simply: business strategy decides what we sell, to whom, and with which profit model. Marketing strategy decides how we bring it to the right person, with which message, and when.

Business strategy

  • Asks: where do we play, and how do we win?
  • Defines the right client and the core service
  • Builds the profit model and pricing
  • Decides what we will not do
  • Measured by profit and income stability
  • Changes rarely, after deep thinking

Marketing strategy

  • Asks: how do we reach the client we chose?
  • Defines the message and positioning
  • Chooses channels, content and ads
  • Builds a path from visibility to trust to sale
  • Measured by client quality and close rate
  • Tested and adjusted continuously
From practice

A clinic marketing one service while living on another

Working with a young aesthetic clinic, the marketing plan was built around hair treatment as the lead service, with a discounted package. When we sat down with the numbers, it turned out that most of the clinic’s income actually came from injectable treatments for women, and that the discounted package was priced below its real cost.

The problem wasn’t the ads. The ads brought inquiries. The problem was that “where to play” had never been decided from the numbers. So we reordered the picture: one clear main track for women’s treatments, hair as a second track, an end to the discount the business was losing money on, and a follow-up process for consultations before any new advertising decision.

Why the order matters

When a business starts with marketing, it promotes an offer that hasn’t been decided yet. Messages change every week, the wrong clients show up, and success is measured in likes instead of profit. Every unit spent on ads amplifies a decision that was never made.

When business strategy is settled first, marketing becomes much simpler: you know who you are writing for, what you are promising, and which price supports it. In the RIMA Method, attracting clients is stage six. Before it come reality, identity, model, pricing and message.

How to build both, in the right order

First, read the numbers. Where does income actually come from? Which service profits once time and cost are counted?

Second, choose where to play. One clear client, and one core service that leads the business.

Third, decide how to win. Why should this client choose you specifically? Which pricing model reflects the outcome?

Fourth, write down what you won’t do. A service to stop, a client who isn’t a fit, a discount not to repeat.

Fifth, only now build the marketing. One message, one or two channels, and a clear path after the first inquiry.

Exercise: a one-page strategy

  1. Write one line: I work with ... who want ...
  2. Write the service that leads your business, and why, in numbers.
  3. Write three things you will stop doing from now on.
  4. Write one sentence: why does this client choose me?
  5. Only then, review your last five posts or ads: do they serve this page or not?

Key takeaways

  • Business strategy decides what, for whom and how we profit. Marketing decides how we reach them.
  • The essence of strategy is choice, including what we won’t do.
  • Marketing before strategy amplifies chaos rather than fixing it.
  • The numbers are the starting point, not the feeling.

Ask yourself today: do I know where I play and how I win? If the answer isn’t clear, the next ad won’t answer it.

Sources: A.G. Lafley & Roger L. Martin, Playing to Win, Harvard Business Review Press, 2013. Michael E. Porter, “What Is Strategy?”, Harvard Business Review, 1996. Philip Kotler & Kevin Lane Keller, Marketing Management.
