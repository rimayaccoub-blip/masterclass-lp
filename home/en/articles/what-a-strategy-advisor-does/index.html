What does a strategy advisor actually do?

Rima Yaqub, November 2026, 8 min read

“Why would I need an advisor?” It’s a fair question. Information is everywhere today, courses are plentiful, and AI answers any question in seconds. So what does someone who sits with you on your business actually add?

Information isn’t the problem

Most owners I work with know a lot. They have read about pricing, watched content on marketing and tried many tools. The problem isn’t what they know. It’s that they are inside the business, and the view from inside is narrow.

From the inside, everything looks urgent. The unanswered message, the client who cancelled, the ad that didn’t work. It’s hard to see the pattern repeating underneath it all.

An advisor doesn’t add more information. An advisor adds distance: an outside view of the numbers and decisions that reveals what can’t be seen from within.

Three things that define strategic advisory

It starts with diagnosis, not solutions. Before any recommendation, we understand the numbers, services and clients, the way a doctor examines before prescribing. General advice like “raise your price” or “post more” can be right for one business and harmful for another.

It helps you choose. Michael Porter writes that the essence of strategy is choosing what not to do. That is the hardest thing for an owner to do alone: which service to stop, which client isn’t a fit, which opportunity to turn down.

It stays through execution. A plan alone changes nothing. Partnership means weekly follow-up, adjusting when something doesn’t work, and measuring the numbers every month. That is the difference between a course you watch and a partnership in which you build a system.

An example from business

LEGO: saved by an outside view

In the early 2000s LEGO was losing money and heading towards collapse, after expanding in many directions: clothing, video games, theme parks and thousands of new pieces. The CEO who led the turnaround, Jørgen Vig Knudstorp, came to the company from consulting at McKinsey.

His first move wasn’t a new campaign. It was an honest diagnosis of the numbers, followed by hard decisions about what the company would no longer do: selling the theme parks, cutting products and returning to the core brick. Within a few years LEGO was profitable again. That is exactly what a strategic view adds: clarity in the numbers, and courage in choosing.

What working with a strategy advisor looks like

Diagnosis. Reading the numbers, services and client journey, and finding where profit leaks or where sales stall.

Decisions. Choosing the client, the core service, the pricing model, and what stops.

Build. Packages, messages, a sales path and simple follow-up tools that can actually be run.

Measure. A monthly review: what changed in the numbers, and what we adjust.

Questions to ask before you start with any advisor

Do they start with diagnosis or with a ready-made solution? If you hear the solution before they ask about your numbers, it’s general advice, not advisory.

Do they understand numbers? A strategy that never touches cash flow and profitability stays a nice conversation.

Do they stay through execution? Ask what weekly follow-up looks like, and how progress is measured.

Will they tell you “no” when needed? A good advisor will sometimes tell you that the step you want isn’t the right one now, even when that’s less comfortable.

When you need an advisor

When the business is established and income exists, but it has hit a ceiling. When you work more without earning more. When inquiries arrive but don’t turn into clients. And when you have tried many fixes and nothing fundamental has changed.

On the other hand, if the business is still an idea without clients, what you likely need first is to test it in the market, not a full strategic partnership.

Exercise: do you need an outside view?

  1. Write down the problem you have been trying to solve for more than three months.
  2. Write down everything you have tried to solve it.
  3. Ask: were all these attempts of the same kind? (For example, all marketing.)
  4. Write three numbers about your business you don’t know precisely today.
  5. If the attempts are similar and the numbers are missing, the problem is usually not effort. It is perspective.

Key takeaways

  • A strategy advisor doesn’t add information. They add distance and clarity.
  • Diagnosis comes before any solution, and choosing includes what you won’t do.
  • The real value lies in staying through execution and measurement, not in the plan alone.
  • Many sales problems are solved with a clear path, not another ad.

Good advisory doesn’t hand you ready-made answers. It gives you the clarity to make the right decision, and the system to carry it out.

Source: Michael E. Porter, “What Is Strategy?”, Harvard Business Review, 1996. David C. Robertson, Brick by Brick, Crown Business, 2013.
