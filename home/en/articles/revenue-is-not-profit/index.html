Revenue is not profit

Rima Yaqub, November 2026, 7 min read

The sentence I hear most from service business owners is: “Money comes in, but I don’t feel it.” It isn’t an exaggeration. In many businesses, revenue grows year after year while profit stays exactly where it was, or quietly shrinks.

Do you know today, precisely, how much you keep from each service you sell? Not how much comes in, how much stays. If the answer takes a long time, this piece is for you.

Why it happens

Service business owners usually look at one number: how much came in this month. It’s easy, it shows up in the bank account, and it feels like progress. The harder questions go unanswered: what did this service cost me? How many hours did it really take? Where exactly did this month’s profit come from?

The reason isn’t carelessness. Most of us were never taught to read our business as a financial system. We learn the profession, master it, open a business, and assume revenue will translate itself into profit. It doesn’t, not on its own.

Three places where profit disappears

Services that look profitable and aren’t. The service in highest demand can be the least profitable once real time, materials, preparation and follow-up are counted. High demand is not high margin.

Time that never made it into the price. The one-hour session has quietly become three hours of messages, revisions and follow-up, while the price stayed the same. That time is only free on paper.

Offers and discounts nobody calculated. A “special” package to attract clients, or a one-time discount that became a habit. Each is small; together they can consume an entire margin.

An example from business

WeWork: revenue doubling, losses growing with it

When WeWork filed to go public in 2019, its revenue had doubled in a single year to about $1.8 billion. On the surface, a success story. But in that same year its losses exceeded $1.6 billion, roughly the size of its entire revenue.

Every new location raised revenue, and with it commitments and costs. Once investors read the numbers in detail, the IPO was pulled and leadership changed. The lesson isn’t only for giant companies: revenue that grows without leaving profit behind isn’t growth. It is a burden that grows.

The financial file: a simple tool that changes decisions

Every engagement I run starts with what I call the business’s financial file. Not complex accounting software, but a clear picture of each service, built on four questions:

1. Price: what the client actually pays, after discounts and offers.

2. Real time: how many hours the service takes from the first message to the last follow-up, not just the session itself.

3. Direct cost: materials, tools, commissions, and any expense tied to that specific service.

4. Monthly volume: how many times the service sells in a month.

Put these numbers in one table and something appears that no bank statement shows: profit per hour for each service. Often the surprise is that the service that exhausts you most is the one that earns least.

Exercise: one hour with your numbers

  1. Write down your three main services.
  2. For each: actual price, total time in hours, and direct cost.
  3. Calculate (price minus cost) divided by hours. That is your profit per hour.
  4. Rank the services from highest to lowest. Which one carries the business? Which one drains it?
  5. Choose one decision for this month: raise a price, rebuild a service as a package, or stop an offer that doesn’t earn.

Beyond the numbers

Numbers alone aren’t enough, but they are the ground every later decision stands on: pricing, packages, and where to invest in marketing. Once you know which service earns, you know what to promote, to whom, and at what price.

Key takeaways

  • Revenue is one number. Profit is the whole story: price, time, cost and volume.
  • The most requested service isn’t necessarily the most profitable one.
  • Uncounted time and uncalculated offers are where margin leaks most.
  • A simple financial file per service reveals in an hour what a year didn’t.

Real growth doesn’t start with a new ad. It starts with knowing precisely what stays in your hands.

Sources: The We Company (WeWork), Form S-1 filing, August 2019.
