Price the outcome, not the hour

Rima Yaqub, November 2026, 7 min read

The same service sells for 100 with one provider and 1,000 with another. In most cases the difference isn’t skill. It’s what each of them is actually selling.

The first sells time: an hour, a session, a treatment. The second sells an outcome: a clear change whose value the client understands before paying. That is where the income gap begins, and the exhaustion gap too.

Why per-session pricing becomes a ceiling

When price is tied to the session, income is capped by the hours in a day, and every attempt to grow means more work, not more profit. At some point the business hits a ceiling: the calendar is full, the body is tired, and income doesn’t move.

Harder still, per-session pricing makes the client decide session by session. Every meeting is a new buying decision, and every new decision is another chance to stop halfway, before reaching the outcome they came for.

An example from business

Rolls-Royce: don’t sell an engine, sell an hour of flight

In 1962 Rolls-Royce introduced a model it called “Power by the Hour”. Instead of selling the engine, spare parts and maintenance separately, it charged a fixed rate for every hour the engine flew, and took responsibility for maintenance and replacement itself.

The operator was no longer buying equipment. It was buying the outcome it cared about: an engine that works and a cost it can predict. And the manufacturer now had every reason to build a better engine, because its profit was tied to the outcome, not to the number of parts sold. The model is still in use today.

A simple pricing formula

Price is built from three elements: the value of the outcome to the client, the risk it helps them avoid, and the urgency they feel. Ask yourself three questions: how much does the client save or gain after the service? What risk do they avoid? How much pain goes away?

These questions don’t hand you a ready number, but they shift the conversation from “how many hours” to “what is the change worth”. That is where a price becomes defensible.

From sessions to a path: four steps

1. Define the outcome. What changes for the client at the end of working with you? One sentence, in the client’s language, not your profession’s.

2. Map the route. How many stages does it take to reach that outcome? How many sessions, over what time? That is the path.

3. Name the path. One clear name and one price for the whole path, with instalments if needed. No price list of sessions.

4. Add what comes after. Maintenance, follow-up or a recurring plan for clients who reached the outcome and want to keep it. That is where recurring income is born.

Exercise: turn one service into a path

  1. Choose the service you sell most often by the session.
  2. Write the outcome the client actually wants, in their words.
  3. How many sessions does it take to get there? Over how many weeks?
  4. Give the path a name and a single price, and compare it with the total of the sessions today.
  5. Try presenting it to your next two clients at the end of the assessment.

Raising prices starts inside

Many owners fear raising prices because they justify them with effort. Once price rests on a clear outcome and a clear path, the justifying stops, and the conversation is about the change, not the number.

Key takeaways

  • Per-session pricing caps income at your hours and makes the client decide again every time.
  • Sometimes the problem isn’t too few clients, it’s clients who don’t complete.
  • A path has an outcome, a name, one price, and what comes after.
  • Price is built on outcome value, risk and urgency, not on hours.

Don’t ask how many hours your service is worth. Ask what the change you create is worth.

Sources: Rolls-Royce, “50th anniversary of Power-by-the-Hour”, press release, 2012.
