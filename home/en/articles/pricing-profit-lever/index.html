Pricing: the profit lever few owners pull

Rima Yaqub, November 2026, 7 min read

When owners want more profit, they think about more clients first. They rarely think about price, even though the numbers say price is the strongest profit lever, by a wide margin.

Have you raised your prices in the past year? If not, what actually stopped you?

What the research says

In a study published in Harvard Business Review in 1992, McKinsey’s Michael Marn and Robert Rosiello analysed data from 2,463 companies. The result: a price improvement of just 1%, with volume unchanged, raised operating profit by about 11% on average.

11%increase in operating profit from a price improvement of just 1%, compared with about 7.8% from cutting variable costs by the same amount, and about 3.3% from growing volume by 1%.

Why the difference? Because every unit added to the price goes straight to profit; the cost hasn’t changed. Growing volume, on the other hand, brings costs with it: more time, more materials, more effort.

That is why Warren Buffett said in a 2010 interview that the single most important decision in evaluating a business is its pricing power: can it raise prices without losing clients to a competitor?

Why we avoid raising prices

In service businesses, price feels personal. The owner feels they are pricing themselves, not their service. So they justify, apologise and offer a discount before anyone asks.

There is another fear: “If I raise the price, clients will leave.” But the math says something different. When margins are thin, a business can lose some clients after a considered price increase and still earn more, with more time for the clients who stayed.

From practice

From apologising to valuing

It’s a pattern I see again and again among service providers: years of experience, ongoing training, real results for clients, and a price that stayed where it was at the start. When the price question comes, an apology comes with it.

One participant wrote to me after we worked on pricing: “You taught me to charge more because of my expertise and the work I’ve put into myself, to stop apologising, and to value myself properly.” The change didn’t start with the number. It started with understanding the value she delivers, then translating it into an offer and a price she could state with confidence.

How to raise prices deliberately

1. Start with the numbers. How much do you actually earn from each service? Without that figure, any price is a guess.

2. Define the value. What outcome does the client get? How much do they save or gain? What risk do they avoid?

3. Start with new clients. Apply the new price to new clients first, and give existing clients advance notice and a clear transition period.

4. Change the offer with the price. A price increase is easier when it comes with a clearer path or an outcome-based package, not just a new number on the same service.

5. Practise the sentence. Say the price out loud, without justification and without an automatic discount. Confidence in a price is heard before it is understood.

Exercise: a pricing power check

  1. When did you last raise your price? How much has your expertise grown since then?
  2. Among your last ten clients, how often did you offer a discount nobody asked for?
  3. If you raised your price by 10%, how many clients could you lose before profit drops? Calculate it.
  4. Write the new price sentence exactly as you will say it to your next client, without a single word of apology.

Key takeaways

  • A small price improvement moves profit more than the same improvement in volume or costs.
  • Pricing power is the ability to raise prices without losing clients.
  • The fear of raising prices is often emotional; the math tells another story.
  • A new price needs clear value, a clearer offer, and a sentence said with confidence.

Before you look for a new client, review the price of the clients you already have.

Sources: Michael V. Marn & Robert L. Rosiello, “Managing Price, Gaining Profit”, Harvard Business Review, 1992. Warren Buffett, interview with the Financial Crisis Inquiry Commission (FCIC), 2010.
